The battery is the tool that dies

A cordless tool is sold as a twenty year purchase, and the sticker prices the body. The motor, the chuck, and the switch outlast any garage career. The pack clipped to the bottom is the part with a calendar on it, and the calendar runs about four years whether the drill does or not.

Price the kit. $179 buys the drill, the charger, and two packs. The cells inside are lithium, and they age from heat and from sitting on the charger, not just from cycles. Year four the first pair is weak, year eight the replacements fade. An OEM pack runs $79, and a twenty year tool eats four of them: $316 in packs alone.

The ledger totals. $179 plus $316 is $495 to keep one drill alive for twenty years, $24.75 a year. That is 2.8 times the price of the kit the sticker advertised. The battery spend alone is $316, which is three bare $99 drills with $19 left over. The consumable was never the blades or the bits, it is the platform.

The pack hides the failure mode. An 18V pack is five 3.6V cells wired in a series string, so one cell that quits retires the other four working cells with it. One fault retires a $79 pack, $15.80 per cell thrown away. The bag on a vacuum, the filter on a purifier, the pack under a drill: the throwaway part is the one the sticker never mentions.

The tell sits in the catalog. Bare tools are priced lower than kits because the brand knows which part comes back. Buy the platform, price the packs, and the cordless aisle reads like a razor and blade counter. Cost per decade prices what fails, and in a cordless shop what fails is the pack.
